What we do

Bi-Annual Vision Correction Events

Twice a year, we hold camps offering checkups, diagnosis, corrective lenses and surgery at no or minimal cost to the patient. Our leprosy camp reaches people affected by leprosy, who face some of the steepest barriers to basic healthcare.

Each camp serves close to 600 patients, and about 150–200 of them go on to cataract surgery.

Holistic Support

Beyond medical care, we see the deep toll that vision loss and stigma take on our patients. Many are elderly and isolated, and have lived for decades with poor sight, leprosy or poverty.

These hardships have cut them off from friends, family and the support they need. Depression can also keep them from hoping for a better life or reaching out for help that is available.

So our chapters of local volunteers check in on patients all year round. This support runs informally, on the volunteers' goodwill and their trust in our commitment to the cause.

Fundraising

Each camp needs Rs. 1.5 to 2 lakhs from start to finish. We raise this all year from well-wishers, so that every patient's needs are covered. Any amount is a huge help, and donors can also sponsor one patient's food and lens, or their surgery.

Who are we?

▶Watch our storyVideo on YouTube

The Lens Project, founded by Ilina Mukherjee in 2021, is a non-profit working to improve medical care for the underserved in rural Bengal, hand in hand with village councils and hospitals.

We run vision correction programs that bring free eye care to underprivileged senior citizens and people affected by leprosy: checkups, diagnosis, corrective lenses and cataract surgery, at no or minimal cost to the patient.

Our team brings together doctors from our partner hospital, Vivekananda Mission Ashram Eye Hospital, Midnapur, West Bengal, whose 15 medical staff support every camp, along with village youth, village councillors and local volunteers. A core group of 15 village youth leads the work on the ground.

We focus on small rural communities where people lack not only resources but awareness. Many don't know the healthcare schemes meant for them, or can't navigate them because of illiteracy, stigma or paperwork. India's health system has an enormous task in serving over a billion people, and some elders have fallen too far behind to reach it on their own. We believe care must go to them – not the other way around.

Since 2021, we have assessed more than 4,500 people and supported more than 800 cataract and eye surgeries, with the help of the Gram Panchayat, local health workers and kind-hearted donors. In 2026, The Lens Project received the Diana Award.

Preventable Blindness in Marginalized Communities

India carries one of the world's heaviest burdens of preventable blindness. Despite real progress in healthcare, millions – especially the elderly, the rural poor and people affected by leprosy – still lack basic eye care. For them, even a treatable condition like cataract can mean permanent, life-altering vision loss.

#1

Cataracts and Aging

Cataracts are the leading cause of blindness in India, especially among those aged 50 and above. Although cataract surgery is simple and highly effective, it remains out of reach for large parts of the rural population due to:

  • Lack of awareness
  • Distance from hospitals
  • High cost of private care
  • Overburdened or inaccessible government schemes

66%+ of blindness in people aged 50+ is due to cataracts.1

#2

Leprosy and Neglect

Though declared "eliminated" in 2005, leprosy has not been eradicated in India. Over 1.14 lakh new cases were reported in 2019–20 (WHO).2 Thousands of survivors, particularly in colonies like Nimpura (near Kharagpur), continue to live with:

  • Residual physical disabilities
  • Ocular complications like lagophthalmos, corneal ulcers, and blindness
  • Lifelong stigma and social exclusion

Despite being officially recognized as persons with disabilities, leprosy-affected individuals face massive gaps in access to eye care, including:

  • Government schemes that require difficult certifications
  • No integration of eye care into long-term leprosy support
  • Mobile health units that rarely reach remote colonies
  • No structured follow-up care or disability rehabilitation
#3

Invisible Barriers

Most patients we see are not just underserved; they are left out entirely. They:

  • Cannot afford surgery
  • Do not know such services exist
  • Struggle to leave their homes due to stigma or mobility issues

Many have gone decades without being seen – literally and figuratively.

#4

The Cost of Inaction

Vision loss is linked to poverty, isolation, and depression.

$411 billion

The global cost of lost productivity due to vision impairment, per year.3

We believe sight is a right, and access should never depend on geography, illness, or income. Let us change that.

1 National Blindness and Visual Impairment Survey 2015–19, Government of India. 2 WHO, as reported by the National Leprosy Eradication Programme. 3 Lancet Global Health Commission on Global Eye Health, 2021 (estimate for 2018).

How do we run our programs?

Every camp takes careful planning and logistics. Each one serves close to 600 patients, and about 150–200 of them go on to cataract surgery.

The Lens Project

For medical expertise, we partner with a local hospital, whose doctors treat patients and bring the equipment and medicines. The Lens Project takes care of everything else – planning, logistics, organization and funding – through fundraising and volunteers.

  1. Plan

    We coordinate with the Gram Panchayat and local health workers, who help us reach patients and open up school buildings and community halls. Leaflets go out a few weeks before each camp.

  2. Screen

    Villagers arrive early to register and have their vitals checked. After dilating drops, nurses do a first checkup and the specialist doctor screens each patient.

  3. Treat

    Glasses are handed out where possible. About 200 patients per camp are booked for cataract surgery; volunteers give each a date and transport, and bring them safely home.

  4. Follow up

    For the next month, volunteers check on every patient's well-being and answer any questions they have.

Program Day Setup, Materials & Process

Logistics
  • Tent and pandal setup for patients, with tables and medical stations for examinations
  • Volunteer support for the entire process, including food and water for villagers
  • Post-program support for surgery patients, including transportation to hospitals and post-surgery checkups
  • Physical and emotional support for senior citizens
  • Adequate number of volunteers on program day and for follow-up days
Medical support
  • A team of doctors, nurses, and an ophthalmologist
  • Diagnostic tools including vision charts, slit lamps, autorefractors, and intraocular pressure measuring devices
  • Medicines and eye drops for both general treatment and post-operative care
  • Corrective lenses (pre-made in common powers), distributed immediately where possible

We work closely with Vivekananda Mission Ashram Eye Hospital, Midnapur, West Bengal, for medical support, surgical scheduling, and post-surgery follow-ups.

Post-program day support
  • Patients earmarked for cataract surgery (approximately 200 on average) are given a date and means of transport by volunteers
  • After surgery, they are safely returned to their homes
  • Volunteers check in on their well-being for the next month and answer any questions they may have
